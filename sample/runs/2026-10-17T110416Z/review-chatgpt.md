## Confidential comments to the editor

I recommend rejection. The topic is important and well aligned with Organization Science, and the use of randomized pitch-deck experiments is promising. However, the central empirical and theoretical claim is not currently supportable. The most serious issue is the internal inconsistency in Study 2’s mediation evidence, detailed in Critique 1: the reported a and b paths imply an indirect effect with the opposite sign from the one reported. In addition, Study 2 does not manipulate the same construct as Study 1, so it cannot explain the inclusion effect established in Study 1, as detailed in Critique 2. These problems undermine the article’s main conclusion. The manuscript would also need substantial redesign or reanalysis to address the added-slide confound, measurement overlap between mediator and outcomes, sampling claims, nesting structure, and transparency problems detailed below.

## Comments to the author

This manuscript examines whether including financial deal terms in startup pitch decks affects investor evaluations, and it attempts to explain the effect through ambiguity aversion relative to six alternative mechanisms. The research question is important, practically meaningful, and appropriate for Organization Science. The use of pitch-deck experiments with repeated investor evaluations is also a promising approach.

At the same time, I do not think the manuscript is ready for publication. The core contribution depends on connecting Study 1’s inclusion manipulation to Study 2’s mediation model, but Study 2 manipulates high versus low numerical framing rather than inclusion versus omission. More importantly, the reported signs in the Study 2 mediation model are internally inconsistent: the a and b paths reported in the manuscript imply an indirect effect opposite in sign to the one reported. These issues, along with concerns about construct measurement, statistical modeling, and the consistency of the conclusions, prevent me from having confidence in the manuscript’s central claims.

1. **Page 23, Study 2 Results and Tables 10–12.** The mediation results appear mathematically inconsistent with the reported a and b paths. On the same page, the manuscript states that the manipulation “significantly reduced perceived ambiguity (β = −0.27, t = −4.21, p < .001)” and that perceived ambiguity negatively predicted outcomes, including “Success Odds (β = −0.21, t = −3.86, p < .001).” A negative a path multiplied by a negative b path should yield a positive indirect effect, not the negative indirect effects reported in Table 12. This sign problem is not a minor reporting issue because it changes the substantive interpretation: the text says higher numerical framing reduces ambiguity and ambiguity reduces favorability, which implies higher framing should improve favorability through ambiguity. Yet Table 9 and Table 12 present negative associations with favorability. The manuscript’s central mediation claim cannot be evaluated until this inconsistency is resolved.
   > For Success Odds, the indirect effect was −0.06, 95% CI [−0.10, −0.03]

2. **Page 20, Study 2 Methods.** Study 2 does not test the mechanism for the effect established in Study 1. Study 1 manipulates whether a deal-terms slide is included or omitted, whereas Study 2 manipulates high versus low numerical values on a deal-terms slide. This is a different causal contrast. As a result, Study 2 cannot establish that perceived ambiguity mediates the effect of “deal-term inclusion” unless inclusion itself is manipulated in Study 2 or the link between the two manipulations is otherwise justified. The hypothesis language on page 19 refers to “deal-term inclusion,” but the method tests “numerical framing.” This disconnect weakens the theoretical and causal chain across the two studies.
   > The independent variable was the randomized numerical framing of the deal terms, dummy-coded (0 = low, 1 = high).

3. **Page 24, General Discussion.** The conclusions oscillate between saying deal-term disclosure reduces investor favorability and saying quantitative clarity improves investor responses. For example, page 25 says “Quantitative disclosure reduced the sense of informational vagueness surrounding an early-stage venture, and this reduction in perceived ambiguity, in turn, increased investors’ willingness to engage,” while page 28 concludes that founders can elicit more favorable responses “By clarifying, rather than concealing ambiguity.” These statements conflict with the quoted claim from page 24 and with the Study 1 finding that inclusion reduces favorability. The manuscript needs a single coherent account of whether disclosure increases or decreases investor favorability and under what causal contrast.
   > including quantitative deal terms in startup pitch decks systematically reduces investor favorability, and that this effect operates through perceived ambiguity

4. **Page 8, Study 1 Methods.** The Study 1 treatment confounds deal-term disclosure with adding an extra slide. Because the control decks are original decks and the treatment decks contain an added slide, the estimated effect could reflect deck length, interruption of narrative flow, perceived artificiality, design inconsistency, or the salience of any additional information rather than financial-term disclosure per se. A stronger design would include a placebo added-slide condition or otherwise equalize slide count and attention across conditions. Without this, the causal interpretation “deal-term disclosure reduces investor favorability” is less secure.
   > we added a single slide within the deck that included quantitative information on deal terms

5. **Page 46, Appendix Table S1.** The focal mediator is contaminated with the outcome. One ambiguity-aversion item directly asks whether ambiguity reduced “my willingness to invest,” which is conceptually very close to the dependent variables “Next Steps” and “Share with Others.” This overlap can mechanically inflate mediation estimates because the mediator partly measures the same evaluative response that it is supposed to explain. More broadly, the construct is labeled “ambiguity aversion,” but the items mostly measure perceived ambiguity, trust, confidence, and reluctance rather than a preference over known versus unknown probabilities. The theory and measurement need to distinguish perceived ambiguity from aversion to ambiguity and from investment favorability.
   > Ambiguous information about this deck reduced my willingness to invest.
   *Rows: Development of Theory, Appropriateness of Design, Appropriateness of Analysis and Interpretation, Legitimacy of Conclusions*

6. **Page 50, Appendix Table S1.** Several alternative mediator measures appear to overlap with perceived ambiguity, making the comparison among mechanisms hard to interpret. The decision-avoidance items repeatedly refer to “missing or vague key metrics,” which is very close to the ambiguity construct. Similarly, the cognitive-load items on page 48 include statements such as “I relied on my initial impressions rather than deeply evaluating all the deck’s details,” which measure heuristic processing more than cognitive load. If the competing mediators are not cleanly measured, the conclusion that ambiguity is the “single dominant pathway” is premature.
   > For this deck, missing or vague key metrics made me want to delay my evaluation.

7. **Page 8, Study 1 Methods.** The manuscript repeatedly describes the evidence as involving real investor evaluations, but the qualification threshold for Study 1 is minimal and the investor sample is not described in enough detail. Having “previously evaluated at least one early-stage startup” could include a wide range of experience levels, and the manuscript does not report how many participants were active angels, venture capitalists, accelerator mentors, analysts, or otherwise professionally involved in investing. The issue becomes stronger in Study 2, where page 20 states that participants were “recruited through Prolific.” The conclusions should be narrowed, or the manuscript should provide more detailed evidence that the samples represent the investor population to which the theory is meant to apply.

8. **Page 10, Study 1 Results.** The statistical models do not appear to fully reflect the repeated and crossed structure of the data. Each respondent evaluates multiple decks, and each startup deck is evaluated by many respondents. Clustering only by respondent handles one source of dependence but not shared deck-level shocks or treatment heterogeneity across startups. The company-control models help with mean differences across decks, but they do not substitute for appropriate uncertainty estimation under repeated observations by deck. Study 2 has a related issue: page 22 says models use “random intercepts for participants,” but the pitches are again repeatedly evaluated. The analysis should consider cross-classified models, deck fixed effects with appropriate clustering, or other specifications that match the experimental assignment and dependence structure.
   > Ordinary least squares regressions with robust standard errors clustered by respondent
   *Rows: Appropriateness of Design, Appropriateness of Analysis and Interpretation*

Given that the R-squared values are small, readers need transparent effect sizes, outcome means, and uncertainty estimates to assess substantive importance.
